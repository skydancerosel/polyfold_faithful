## Part I. The sc-calculus (HWZ Chapter 1, §§1.1–1.4)

Chapter 1 of Part I fixes the vocabulary of the whole theory: sc-Banach spaces, sc-operators, sc-subspaces and quotients, sc-Fredholm operators and their stability under $\mathrm{sc}^+$-perturbations, partial quadrants, $\mathrm{sc}^0$ and $\mathrm{sc}^1$ maps, the tangent construction and the inductive definitions of $\mathrm{sc}^k$ and sc-smooth, the relation between sc-differentiability and classical differentiability on the levels (Propositions 1.2.1–1.2.4 (preprint 1.16–1.19) and Corollary 1.2.5 (preprint 1.20), quoted from the 2010 paper), the chain rule, the degeneracy index of a point of a partial quadrant and boundary recognition. The formal proof establishes all of it, Proposition 1.2.4 (preprint 1.19) and Corollary 1.2.5 (preprint 1.20) under the text's own hypothesis ($C^{l+1}$ from $U_{m+l}$ to $F_m$ for $l \le k$), by the 2010 paper's induction $(\mathrm S_k)$ on elementary maps (§I.17). Example 1.1.3 (preprint 1.2), the weighted Sobolev spaces, is not formalized.

Two decisions shape everything. First, an sc-Banach space is an *indexed family* of Banach spaces $E_m$ with injective compact inclusions $E_{m+1} \to E_m$, not one space with a chain of subspaces (§I.1). "The point $x$ of $E_0$ lies in $E_m$" then means that $x$ is in the image of $E_m$, and every map has level maps $f_m : E_m \to F_m$ determined by injectivity. Second, the text's standing hypothesis "$U$ relatively open in a partial quadrant" enters the proofs of §§1.2–1.3 only through two facts about each level set $U_m$: it is the intersection of an open and a convex set, and derivatives within it are unique. These two facts make up the notion of an *sc-domain* (§I.10), which the tangent construction and the level shift preserve by a two-line argument, so the inductions of §1.2 run on sc-domains, and the text's tuples $(U, C, E)$ are a special case.

Four proofs take a different road from the text's. The chain rule replaces the two integrals of §1.4.2 by the one-dimensional mean value inequality along a segment, and does not need the first integral at all (§I.12). The compactness argument behind Propositions 1.2.1–1.2.3 (preprint 1.16–1.18), the subsequence $h_n \to h$ in $E_m$ for unit vectors $h_n \in E_{m+1}$, is isolated once as the *compact-open lemma*, and its $C^k$ version replaces the multilinear maps $\Gamma(x)$ and the differentiation under the integral sign of the 2010 paper (§I.13). Theorem 1.3.5 (preprint 1.25), which the text quotes from another paper, is proved by the argument of §2.4 (preprint §2.3), for $\mathrm{sc}^1$ germs (§I.19). And the induction $(\mathrm S_k)$ behind Proposition 1.2.4 (preprint 1.19) runs on the order $s$ over all elementary maps $d^jf(\pi_0 z)(\pi_1 z, \dots, \pi_j z)$ at once, rather than on a description of the $2^{k+1}$ components of $T^{k+1}f$ (§I.17). Smaller departures are flagged where they occur: the complement in Proposition 1.1.11 (preprint 1.10) is built from a right inverse rather than a lifted basis; the regularizing step of §1.4.1 is a bootstrap; $k = k'$ in Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24) is derived from the lemma, not proved first. What Mathlib lacked, the Riesz–Schauder theorem, the stability of Fredholm operators under compact perturbations and the invariance of the Fredholm index, is proved in §I.7; the index statements of the exposition's Lemma 2.D and Proposition 1.1.11 (preprint 1.10) follow in §I.6 and §I.9. The lemmas that the exposition adds to make the text's remarks precise are proved where they belong: uniqueness of the sc-structure in finite dimensions (§I.1), sc-splittings as sc-projections (§I.3), the smooth points of a quotient (§I.5), the shift and tangent of a tuple (§I.10), compact-open continuity and sc¹ = $C^1$ on quadrants in finite dimensions (§I.11).

### I.1 Scales and sc-Banach spaces (Definition 1.1.1 (preprint 1.1))

*The scale.* A **scale of Banach spaces** consists of real Banach spaces $E_m$, one for each $m \in \mathbb N$, together with continuous linear maps $\mathrm{incl}_m : E_{m+1} \to E_m$ that are injective and compact operators ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale⟧. Compactness is Mathlib's: the preimage of some neighbourhood of $0$ is mapped into a compact set ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator⟧. The composite inclusion $\iota_m : E_m \to E_0$ is defined by recursion, $\iota_0 = \mathrm{id}$ and $\iota_{m+1} = \iota_m \circ \mathrm{incl}_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.toBase⟧; it is injective ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.toBase_injective⟧, and the composite $E_{m+k} \to E_m$ is defined likewise ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.inclK⟧ and is compatible with $\iota$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.toBase_inclK⟧. The text's $E_m$ is here the image $\iota_m(E_m) \subseteq E_0$; the text's $E_m \supset E_{m+1}$ is the inclusion of images, and "$x \in E_m$" for $x \in E_0$ is "$x \in \operatorname{range} \iota_m$". The **smooth points** $E_\infty$ form the subspace of $E_0$ of points that lift to every level, $E_\infty = \bigcap_m \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.smooth⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.mem_smooth_iff⟧, and $E_\infty \cap E_m$ is its preimage in $E_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.smoothLvl⟧; a smooth point of $E_m$ lifts to every $E_{m+k}$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.exists_inclK_of_mem_smoothLvl⟧.

*The density axiom.* An **sc-Banach space** is a scale in which $E_\infty \cap E_m$ is dense in $E_m$ for every $m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach⟧. This is condition (2) of Definition 1.1.1 (preprint 1.1) exactly as the text states it. The weaker statement that $E_{m+k}$ has dense image in $E_m$ follows ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.denseRange_inclK⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.denseRange_incl⟧; it does not obviously imply the axiom, which is why the strong form is the definition.

*Why a family.* Nothing in Chapter 1 uses more than the family: the text's identification of $E_{m+1}$ with a subset of $E_m$ is the injectivity of $\mathrm{incl}_m$, a field of the structure, and its "the inclusion is continuous" is part of compactness. The gain is that each level is a Banach space in its own right, with its own norm, and Mathlib's analysis applies on each level without carrying a filtration along.

*The constant structure.* For a finite-dimensional normed space $V$ the family $E_m = V$ with identity inclusions is a scale: the identity of a finite-dimensional space is a compact operator ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/FiniteDimension · isCompactOperator_id_iff_finiteDimensional⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.constScale⟧. Here $\iota_m$ is the identity ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.constScale_toBase⟧, every point is smooth ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.constScale_smoothLvl⟧, and density is trivial, so $V$ is an sc-Banach space ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.const⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.const_toBase⟧.

*Only the constant structure (Lemma 1.A(2)–(3) of the exposition).* The text remarks that a finite-dimensional space has precisely one sc-structure. For families this reads: if $E$ is an sc-Banach space with $E_0$ finite-dimensional, the inclusions identify $E$ with the constant structure on $E_0$. First, every point is smooth: $E_\infty$ is a subspace of the finite-dimensional $E_0$, hence closed ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/FiniteDimension · Submodule.closed_of_finiteDimensional⟧, and dense by the axiom at level $0$, hence everything ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.smooth_eq_top_of_finiteDimensional⟧. So every $x \in E_0$ lifts to every level, and $\iota_m : E_m \to E_0$, injective by construction, is bijective ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.toBase_bijective_of_finiteDimensional⟧. A continuous linear bijection between Banach spaces is an isomorphism (open mapping theorem) ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Banach · ContinuousLinearEquiv.ofBijective⟧; the isomorphisms $\iota_m$ commute with the inclusions by the very definition $\iota_{m+1} = \iota_m \circ \mathrm{incl}_m$, so they form an sc-isomorphism of $E$ onto the constant structure on $E_0$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isoConstOfFiniteDimensional⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isoConstOfFiniteDimensional_apply⟧. In particular all the norms $|\cdot|_m$ are equivalent to $|\cdot|_0$. The second remark, that on an infinite-dimensional space the constant structure fails the compactness axiom, becomes: if an inclusion $\mathrm{incl}_m : E_{m+1} \to E_m$ of a scale is onto, then $E_m$ is finite-dimensional. Indeed $\mathrm{incl}_m$ is then a continuous linear bijection, hence an isomorphism $e$, and $\mathrm{id}_{E_m} = \mathrm{incl}_m \circ e^{-1}$ is compact as a compact operator composed with a bounded one ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator.comp_clm⟧; a Banach space with compact identity is finite-dimensional ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/FiniteDimension · FiniteDimensional.of_isCompactOperator_id⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScScale.finiteDimensional_of_surjective_incl⟧.

*Direct sums.* For scales $E$, $F$, the family $E_m \times F_m$ with the product inclusions is a scale: the product of two compact operators is compact, because the preimage of a product of neighbourhoods is mapped into the product of two compact sets ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.prodScale⟧. Its $\iota_m$ is the pair of the two ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.prodScale_toBase⟧, and its smooth points are the pairs of smooth points ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.prodScale_smoothLvl⟧; since a product of dense sets is dense, $E \oplus F$ is an sc-Banach space ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.prod⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.prod_smoothLvl⟧.

*The level shift.* The family $E_{m+1}$ with the inclusions $\mathrm{incl}_{m+1}$ is a scale $E^1$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shiftScale⟧. Its composite inclusion to its base $E_1$, followed by $\iota_1$, is $\iota_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shiftScale_toBase⟧, and its smooth points at level $m$ are the smooth points of $E$ at level $m+1$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shiftScale_smoothLvl⟧, so $E^1$ is an sc-Banach space ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shift⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shift_toBase⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shift_smoothLvl⟧; $E^k$ is the $k$-fold iterate ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shiftK⟧.

*Level sets.* For $U \subseteq E_0$ the level set $U_m \subseteq E_m$ is the preimage $\iota_m^{-1}(U)$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.lvlSet⟧; $U_0 = U$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.lvlSet_zero⟧, $U_{m+1} = \mathrm{incl}_m^{-1}(U_m)$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.lvlSet_succ⟧, and level sets preserve inclusions, intersections, openness, closedness and convexity ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.lvlSet_mono⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.lvlSet_inter⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.isOpen_lvlSet⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.isClosed_lvlSet⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · ScBanach.convex_lvlSet⟧. This is the text's filtration $A_m = A \cap E_m$ of a subset. The level sets of $U_1 \subseteq E_1$, taken in the shifted space $E^1$, are the $U_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · ScBanach.shift_lvlSet⟧, which is the text's $(A^1)_m = A_{1+m}$.

### I.2 Sc-operators, sc-isomorphisms, sc⁺-operators (Definitions 1.1.4–1.1.5 (preprint 1.3–1.4))

An **sc-operator** $T : E \to F$ is a family of continuous linear maps $T_m : E_m \to F_m$ commuting with the inclusions, $\mathrm{incl}^F_m \circ T_{m+1} = T_m \circ \mathrm{incl}^E_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator⟧. Then $\iota_m T_m = T_0 \iota_m$ by induction ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.toBase_comm⟧, so the whole family is determined by the base map $T_0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.ext_base⟧: this is the sense in which the text's single linear map $T$ "with $T(E_m) \subset F_m$ and $T : E_m \to F_m$ continuous" is the same thing. The text's formulation is also available as a predicate on a continuous linear $T_0 : E_0 \to F_0$, "for every $m$ there is a continuous linear $T_m$ with $\iota_m T_m = T_0 \iota_m$" ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · IsScOperator⟧, and the two notions pass into each other ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.isScOperator⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · IsScOperator.toScOperator⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · IsScOperator.toScOperator_zero⟧. The identity is an sc-operator, sc-operators compose and add ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.id⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.comp⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.add⟧, and they map smooth points to smooth points, since a lift of $x$ to level $j$ is carried by $T_j$ to a lift of $T_0 x$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScOperator.mapsTo_smoothLvl⟧.

An **sc-isomorphism** is a family of continuous linear equivalences $E_m \simeq F_m$ commuting with the inclusions ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso⟧; it has an underlying sc-operator ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.toScOperator⟧, an inverse ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.symm⟧, composites ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.trans⟧ and the identity ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.refl⟧, and $\iota_m$ commutes with it and with its inverse ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.toBase_comm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScIso.symm_toBase_comm⟧. The text defines an sc-isomorphism as a bijective sc-operator whose inverse is an sc-operator; the continuity of the inverse on each level, which there follows from the open mapping theorem, is here part of the data.

An **$\mathrm{sc}^+$-operator** $E \to F$ is an sc-operator $E \to F^1$ into the shifted space, i.e. continuous linear maps $S_m : E_m \to F_{m+1}$ commuting with the inclusions ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScPlus⟧. The text's first condition, $S(E_m) \subset F_{m+1}$, is contained in the second and is dropped. The inclusion $F^1 \to F$, level by level $\mathrm{incl}_m : F_{m+1} \to F_m$, is itself an sc-operator ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.shiftIncl⟧, and composing gives the sc-operator $E \to F$ underlying $S$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScPlus.toScOperator⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScPlus.toScOperator_apply⟧. Its level-$m$ map is the compact $\mathrm{incl}_m$ after the bounded $S_m$, hence compact ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator.comp_clm⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScPlus.isCompactOperator⟧: the text's remark that $\mathrm{sc}^+$-operators are sc-compact. In terms of images, $S$ raises the level: if $x \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ then $S_0 x \in \operatorname{range}\iota_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScPlus.toScOperator_mem_range_toBase_succ⟧.

### I.3 Sc-subspaces and sc-splittings (Definition 1.1.6 (preprint 1.5))

For a subspace $A \subseteq E_0$ its **level-$m$ part** $A_m \subseteq E_m$ is the preimage of $A$ under $\iota_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.levelSub⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.mem_levelSub⟧; $A_0 = A$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.levelSub_zero⟧, the inclusions map $A_{m+1}$ into $A_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.incl_mem_levelSub⟧, and $A_m$ is closed when $A$ is, as the preimage of a closed set under a continuous map ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.isClosed_levelSub⟧.

$A$ is an **sc-subspace** if $A$ is closed and, for every $m$, $A_m$ lies in the closure of $A_m \cap E_\infty$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.IsScSubspace⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.IsScSubspace.isClosed⟧. The text asks that $(A_m)$ be an sc-structure on $A$; of the conditions in that phrase, the closedness of $A_m$ in $E_m$ was just proved, and the compactness of the restricted inclusions $A_{m+1} \to A_m$ is automatic, since the restriction of a compact operator to a subspace, with values in a closed subspace, is compact. This is how the scale structure on a closed $A$ is built: levels $A_m$, inclusions the restrictions of $\mathrm{incl}_m$, injective because $\mathrm{incl}_m$ is, compact by the remark ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.ofSubspaceScale⟧. Its composite inclusion is that of $E$ on the underlying points ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.ofSubspaceScale_coe_toBase⟧, and its smooth points are the points of $A_m$ that are smooth in $E$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.mem_ofSubspaceScale_smoothLvl⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.ofSubspaceScale_smoothLvl⟧. The density condition of `IsScSubspace` is then exactly the density axiom for this scale, so an sc-subspace is an sc-Banach space with the induced structure ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.ofSubspace⟧.

$E = F \oplus G$ is an **sc-splitting** if $F$ and $G$ are sc-subspaces and, for every $m$, $F_m$ and $G_m$ are complementary subspaces of $E_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.IsScSplitting⟧. The text asks for a *topological* direct sum $E_m = F_m \oplus G_m$. Since $F_m$ and $G_m$ are closed and complementary, the projection onto $F_m$ along $G_m$ is continuous by the closed graph theorem, which is Mathlib's "closed complemented from complementary closed subspaces" ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.closedComplemented⟧; so nothing is lost by asking only for complementarity. The splitting is symmetric in $F$ and $G$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.symm⟧.

*Sc-splittings are sc-projections (Lemma 2.B of the exposition).* The proof of Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) in the text passes from "$P \circ P = P$, continuous on every level" to "a sc-projection" and an sc-complement $(1 - P)(E)$ without comment. Both directions are proved. (i) Given an sc-splitting $E = A \oplus B$, on each level $A_m$ and $B_m$ are complementary and closed, hence topological complements ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Module/Complemented · Submodule.IsCompl.isTopCompl_of_isClosed⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.isTopCompl⟧, so the projection $P_m$ of $E_m$ onto $A_m$ along $B_m$ is continuous ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/Complement · Submodule.projectionL⟧. These projections commute with the inclusions: write $x = P_{m+1}x + (x - P_{m+1}x)$ with the first term in $A_{m+1}$ and the second in $B_{m+1}$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/Complement · Submodule.projectionL_eq_self_sub_projectionL⟧; $\mathrm{incl}_m$ carries them into $A_m$ and $B_m$ (§I.3), where $P_m$ is the identity, resp. zero ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/Complement · Submodule.projectionL_eq_self_iff⟧ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/Complement · Submodule.projectionL_apply_eq_zero_iff⟧, so $P_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x) = \mathrm{incl}_m(P_{m+1}x)$. Hence the $P_m$ form an sc-operator $P$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.scProj⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.scProj_apply⟧ with $P_m^2 = P_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.scProj_idem⟧, $P_m(E_m) = A_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.range_scProj⟧ and $\ker P_m = B_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.IsScSplitting.ker_scProj⟧. (ii) Conversely let $P$ be an sc-operator with $P_0^2 = P_0$. Idempotence passes to every level by injectivity of $\iota_m$, since $\iota_m P_m P_m x = P_0P_0\iota_m x = P_0\iota_m x = \iota_m P_m x$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScOperator.idem_lvl⟧. The level-$m$ part of $P_0(E_0)$ is $P_m(E_m)$: if $\iota_m x = P_0 y$ then $P_0\iota_m x = \iota_m x$, i.e. $\iota_m P_m x = \iota_m x$, so $x = P_m x$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScOperator.levelSub_range_eq⟧; the level-$m$ part of $\ker P_0$ is $\ker P_m$ (§I.6, ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScOperator.ker_lvl⟧). $P_0(E_0) = \{x : P_0x = x\}$ is closed, and $\ker P_0$ is closed. Density of smooth points: $x \in P_m(E_m)$ satisfies $x = P_mx$ and is the limit of $P_m z_n$ for smooth $z_n \to x$, and the $P_m z_n$ are smooth points of $P_m(E_m)$ (§I.2); $x \in \ker P_m$ satisfies $x = x - P_mx$ and is the limit of $z_n - P_mz_n$, smooth points of $\ker P_m$. Finally $P_m(E_m)$ and $\ker P_m$ are complementary because $P_m$ is idempotent ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Projection · LinearMap.IsIdempotentElem.isCompl⟧. So $E = P_0(E_0) \oplus \ker P_0$ is an sc-splitting ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isScSplitting_of_idem⟧, and together: $E = A \oplus B$ is an sc-splitting iff an idempotent sc-operator has range $A$ and kernel $B$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isScSplitting_iff_exists_scProj⟧. The text's $(1 - P)(E)$ is $\ker P$. The formal Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) (§I.4) builds its complement directly and does not use this.

### I.4 Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6): finite-dimensional sc-subspaces

**Statement.** Let $F \subseteq E_0$ be finite-dimensional. Then $F$ is an sc-subspace if and only if $F \le E_\infty$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.proposition_1_1_7_iff⟧; and if $F \le E_\infty$ there is a $G$ with $E = F \oplus G$ an sc-splitting ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.proposition_1_1_7⟧.

**The equivalence.** If $F$ is an sc-subspace, the density condition at level $0$ says $F \subseteq \overline{F \cap E_\infty}$. But $F \cap E_\infty$ is a subspace of the finite-dimensional $F$, hence finite-dimensional, hence closed ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/FiniteDimension · Submodule.closed_of_finiteDimensional⟧; so $F \subseteq F \cap E_\infty$, i.e. $F \le E_\infty$. Conversely, if $F \le E_\infty$ then $F$ is closed (finite-dimensional) and every point of $F_m$ is already a smooth point of $F_m$, so the density condition holds trivially.

**The complement.** Mathlib provides a continuous linear projection $p : E_0 \to F$ with $p|_F = \mathrm{id}$, from the closed complementation of finite-dimensional subspaces (Hahn–Banach) ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/LocallyConvex/HahnBanach · Submodule.ClosedComplemented.of_finiteDimensional⟧. This is the text's $P(x) = \sum \lambda_i(x) e_i$. Put $G := \ker p$. Since $F \le E_\infty$, the restriction of $\iota_m$ to $F_m$ is a bijection onto $F$ (injective by injectivity of $\iota_m$, surjective because every point of $F$ lifts to level $m$) ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.levelRestrict⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.levelRestrict_bijective⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.levelEquiv⟧; through this linear isomorphism $F_m \cong F$ the projection lifts to a continuous linear $P_m : E_m \to F_m$, $P_m = (\iota_m|_{F_m})^{-1} \circ p \circ \iota_m$ (continuous because $F_m$ is finite-dimensional) ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.finProj⟧. It fixes $F_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.finProj_apply_of_mem⟧, and $P_m x = 0$ iff $p(\iota_m x) = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.finProj_eq_zero_iff⟧, so $G_m = \ker P_m$. Now $F$ is an sc-subspace by the first part, and $G$ is closed as a kernel. For the density of $G_m \cap E_\infty$ in $G_m$: given $g \in G_m$, let $Q := 1 - P_m$ on $E_m$; then $Qg = g$, $Q$ maps $E_m$ into $G_m$, and $Q$ maps smooth points to smooth points, since $P_m x$ is smooth ($F \le E_\infty$) and smooth points form a subspace ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScScale.smoothLvl_submodule_closed_sub⟧. Hence $g = Qg$ lies in the closure of $Q(E_\infty \cap E_m) \subseteq G_m \cap E_\infty$, because $E_\infty \cap E_m$ is dense and $Q$ is continuous. This is the text's $g_n = (1 - P)f_n \to g$. Finally $F_m$ and $G_m = \ker P_m$ are complementary because $P_m$ is a projection onto $F_m$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Projection · isCompl_of_proj⟧.

### I.5 Proposition 1.1.8 (preprint 1.7): quotients

**Statement.** Let $A \subseteq E_0$ be closed. The quotients $E_m/A_m$ form an sc-Banach space $E/A$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotient⟧, the quotient maps form an sc-operator $E \to E/A$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotientMap⟧, and, as a statement, there are an sc-Banach space $Q$ and an sc-operator $q : E \to Q$ with every $q_m$ surjective and $\ker q_m = A_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.proposition_1_1_8⟧. The text assumes $A$ is an sc-subspace; only closedness is used.

**The inclusions.** $A_m$ is closed in $E_m$ (§I.3), so $E_m/A_m$ is a Banach space. The map $E_{m+1}/A_{m+1} \to E_m/A_m$ is induced by $\mathrm{incl}_m$: it is well defined because $\mathrm{incl}_m$ maps $A_{m+1}$ into $A_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotIncl⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotIncl_mk⟧. It is injective: if $\mathrm{incl}_m x \in A_m$ then $\iota_{m+1} x = \iota_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x) \in A$, so $x \in A_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotIncl_injective⟧. This injectivity is the text's identification of $E_m/A_m$ with the subspace $\{x + A : x \in E_m\}$ of $E/A$, and it holds precisely because $A_m$ is the *full* preimage of $A$. It is compact: the quotient map $E_m \to E_m/A_m$ after the compact $\mathrm{incl}_m$ is compact, and the induced map on $E_{m+1}/A_{m+1}$ sends the image of a neighbourhood of $0$ (which is a neighbourhood, since quotient maps are open) into the same compact set ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotIncl_isCompactOperator⟧. So the quotients form a scale ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotientScale⟧, whose composite inclusion sends the class of $x$ to the class of $\iota_m x$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotientScale_toBase_mk⟧. The text's argument with a sequence $x_k + A_{m+1}$ of norm $\le 1$ and representatives of norm $\le 2$ is the sequential form of the same fact.

**Density.** The quotient map $E_m \to E_m/A_m$ is continuous and surjective, so the image of the dense set $E_\infty \cap E_m$ is dense. A class $[x]$ with $x$ smooth is a smooth point of the quotient scale: a lift $y$ of $x$ to level $j$ gives the lift $[y]$ of $[x]$. So the smooth points of $E_m/A_m$ are dense ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.quotient⟧. This is the inclusion "$\{x + A : x \in E_\infty\} \subseteq (E/A)_\infty$" of the text's proof, which is all its density claim needs.

**The smooth points of the quotient (Lemma 2.C of the exposition).** The text also asserts that the two sets are equal. Let $A$ be an sc-subspace and $x \in E_0$ with, for every $j$, a point $x_j \in E_j$ such that $\iota_j x_j - x \in A$. We find a smooth $y$ with $y - x \in A$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.exists_smooth_mem_coset⟧. *The differences.* $d_j := \mathrm{incl}_j x_{j+1} - x_j \in E_j$ has $\iota_j d_j = \iota_{j+1}x_{j+1} - \iota_j x_j = (\iota_{j+1}x_{j+1} - x) - (\iota_jx_j - x) \in A$, so $d_j \in A_j$. *A uniform bound for the inclusions.* Put $P_j := \prod_{i<j}\max(1, \|\mathrm{incl}_i\|) \ge 1$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.inclBound⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.one_le_inclBound⟧. By induction, $\|\iota_jv\| \le P_j\|v\|$ for $v \in E_j$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.norm_toBase_le⟧ and $\|\mathrm{incl}^{J+k}_Jv\| \le P_{J+k}\|v\|$ for the composite inclusion $E_{J+k} \to E_J$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.norm_inclK_le⟧, each step multiplying by $\|\mathrm{incl}_i\| \le \max(1, \|\mathrm{incl}_i\|)$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.norm_incl_le⟧. This replaces the exposition's $M_j = \max_{i\le j}\|E_j \to E_i\|$, and avoids comparing levels $J + k$ with $j$. *Smooth corrections.* Since $A$ is an sc-subspace, $A_j \cap E_\infty$ is dense in $A_j$, so there are $c_j \in A_j \cap E_\infty$ with $\|d_j - c_j\| \le 2^{-j}/P_j$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/Pseudo/Defs · Metric.mem_closure_iff⟧. Then $b_j := d_j - c_j \in A_j$ has $P_j\|b_j\| \le 2^{-j}$. *The series.* $\sum_j \iota_jb_j$ converges absolutely in $E_0$, its terms being bounded by $2^{-j}$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Group/InfiniteSum · Summable.of_norm_bounded⟧; let $S$ be its sum and $y := x_0 + S$. Its partial sums lie in $A$, which is closed, so $S \in A$ (the additive form of ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/InfiniteSum/NatInt · HasProd.tendsto_prod_nat⟧), and $y - x = (x_0 - x) + S \in A$. *$y$ is smooth.* Fix $J$. The tail $t := \sum_k \mathrm{incl}^{J+k}_J b_{J+k}$ converges in $E_J$ (terms bounded by $2^{-J}2^{-k}$), and $\iota_J t = \sum_k \iota_{J+k}b_{J+k}$ because $\iota_J$ is continuous and linear ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/InfiniteSum/Module · ContinuousLinearMap.map_tsum⟧ and $\iota_J \circ \mathrm{incl}^{J+k}_J = \iota_{J+k}$ (§I.1). The head telescopes: $\sum_{j<J}\iota_jd_j = \iota_Jx_J - x_0$ (the additive form of ⟦Mathlib/Algebra/BigOperators/Group/Finset/Basic · Finset.prod_range_div⟧), so $\sum_{j<J}\iota_jb_j = (\iota_Jx_J - x_0) - \sum_{j<J}\iota_jc_j$, and the last sum is smooth, hence $\iota_J w$ for some $w \in E_J$. Splitting $S$ into head and tail (the additive form of ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/InfiniteSum/NatInt · Multipliable.prod_mul_tprod_nat_add⟧) gives $y = \iota_J(x_J - w + t)$. As $J$ was arbitrary, $y \in E_\infty$. *As a statement about $E/A$.* The class of $x$ is a smooth point of $E/A$ iff for every $j$ it is the image of a class $[z]$, $z \in E_j$, i.e. iff $\iota_jz - x \in A$ for some $z$ (§I.5 and ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Quotient/Defs · Submodule.Quotient.eq⟧); by the above this happens iff $x + A$ contains a smooth point ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.mk_mem_quotient_smooth_iff⟧. This is the text's equality.

### I.6 Definition 1.1.9 (preprint 1.8): sc-Fredholm operators

**Level kernels and ranges.** For an sc-operator $T$, the kernel of $T_m$ is the level-$m$ part of the kernel of $T_0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScOperator.ker_lvl⟧, and the range of $T_m$ lies in the level-$m$ part of the range of $T_0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScOperator.range_lvl_le⟧; both from $\iota_m T_m = T_0 \iota_m$ and injectivity.

**The definition.** An **sc-Fredholm splitting** of $T : E \to F$ consists of subspaces $K, X \subseteq E_0$ and $C, Y \subseteq F_0$ such that $E = K \oplus X$ and $F = C \oplus Y$ are sc-splittings, $K = \ker T_0$, $Y = \operatorname{range} T_0$, $K$ and $C$ are finite-dimensional, and $T_m$ restricts to a bijection $X_m \to Y_m$ for every $m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting⟧; $T$ is **sc-Fredholm** if such a splitting exists ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · IsScFredholm⟧. The text's condition (3), "$T : X \to Y$ is an sc-isomorphism", is here the bijectivity on each level; the continuity of the inverses $Y_m \to X_m$ is automatic by the open mapping theorem between the closed subspaces $X_m$ and $Y_m$, so nothing is lost.

**The index.** The index of a splitting is $\dim K - \dim C$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.index⟧, and the index of $T$ is $\dim\ker T_0 - \dim(F_0/\operatorname{range}T_0)$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · scIndex⟧. They agree: $K = \ker T_0$, and $C$ is a complement of $Y = \operatorname{range}T_0$ in $F_0$, so $C \cong F_0/Y$ ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Projection · quotientEquivOfIsCompl⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.index_eq⟧. So the index does not depend on the splitting, which the text does not state.

**The remarks after the definition.** $K$ is a finite-dimensional sc-subspace, hence $K \le E_\infty$ by Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.K_le_smooth⟧. $\ker T_m = K_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.ker_lvl⟧ and $\operatorname{range} T_m = Y_m$: one inclusion from above, the other because $T_m$ maps $X_m$ onto $Y_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.range_lvl⟧. The text's "$F = C \oplus T(E_m)$" (read $F_m$) is the level-$m$ part of the splitting $F = C \oplus Y$ together with $\operatorname{range}T_m = Y_m$. Finally, every $T_m$ is a Fredholm operator in Mathlib's sense: its kernel $K_m$ injects into the finite-dimensional $K$, its range $Y_m$ is closed, and $Y_m$ has the finite-dimensional complement $C_m$ (which injects into $C$), so its range has finite codimension ⟦Mathlib/RingTheory/Finiteness/Cofinite · FG.cofg_of_isCompl⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScFredholmSplitting.isFredholm⟧, using the criterion of §I.7. This is the fact the text uses in the first line of §1.4.1.

**The index on every level (Lemma 2.D of the exposition).** $C$ is a finite-dimensional sc-subspace, so $C \le F_\infty$ by Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · ScFredholmSplitting.C_le_smooth⟧. For any subspace $A \le E_\infty$, $\iota_m$ restricts to a bijection $A_m \to A$ (injective as $\iota_m$ is, surjective because every point of $A$ lifts), so $\dim A_m = \dim A$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · ScBanach.finrank_levelSub_of_le_smooth⟧. Hence $\dim\ker T_m = \dim K_m = \dim K$, and $F_m/\operatorname{range}T_m = F_m/Y_m \cong C_m$ (complements, ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Projection · quotientEquivOfIsCompl⟧) has dimension $\dim C$. So $T_m$ is Fredholm with index $\dim K - \dim C$ in the sense of §I.7 ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · ScFredholmSplitting.fredholmIndex_lvl⟧, which by the previous paragraph is the index of $T$ for every splitting ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · IsScFredholm.fredholmIndex_lvl⟧; on level $0$ this is the definition, $\operatorname{ind}(T)$ being the Fredholm index of $T_0$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · scIndex_eq_fredholmIndex⟧. The text states the splittings on each level but not the index.

### I.7 Fredholm operators between Banach spaces: Riesz–Schauder and compact perturbations

Mathlib has a notion of Fredholm operator ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Fredholm/Basic · IsFredholm⟧: a continuous linear $u : E \to F$ that is a strict map, with closed range, finite-dimensional kernel, range of finite codimension ⟦Mathlib/RingTheory/Finiteness/Cofinite · CoFG⟧ and closed complemented kernel. It does not have the two theorems that §1.4.1 quotes as classical, that $1 + \text{compact}$ is Fredholm and that compact perturbations preserve the property. Both are proved here, for real Banach spaces.

**The criterion.** A continuous linear map with closed range between Banach spaces is a strict map ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Maps/Strict/Basic · IsStrictMap⟧: by the open mapping theorem its corestriction to the range is an open quotient map ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Maps/Strict/Module · isStrictMap_iff_isOpenQuotientMap_rangeRestrict⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isStrictMap_of_isClosed_range⟧. Hence a continuous linear map with finite-dimensional kernel, closed range and range of finite codimension is Fredholm, the kernel being closed complemented because it is finite-dimensional ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_of_banach⟧. A finite-rank operator is compact: the image of the unit ball is bounded in the finite-dimensional range, so its closure is compact ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isCompactOperator_of_finiteDimensional_range⟧.

**Riesz–Schauder: the kernel.** Let $c : E \to E$ be compact and $N := \ker(1 + c)$. On $N$ the operator $c$ is $-\mathrm{id}$, and $c$ restricts to a compact operator of the closed subspace $N$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator.restrict⟧; so $\mathrm{id}_N$ is compact, and $N$ is finite-dimensional ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/FiniteDimension · FiniteDimensional.of_isCompactOperator_id⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · finiteDimensional_ker_one_add⟧.

**The range is closed.** Take a closed complement $G$ of $N$ (which exists since $N$ is finite-dimensional). $1 + c$ is bounded below on $G$: otherwise there are unit vectors $g_n \in G$ with $(1 + c)g_n \to 0$; the $c g_n$ lie in a compact set ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator.image_closedBall_subset_compact⟧, so along a subsequence $c g_n \to y$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Sequences · IsCompact.tendsto_subseq⟧, hence $g_n = (1+c)g_n - cg_n \to -y$; then $-y \in G$ (closed), $\|-y\| = 1$, and $(1+c)(-y) = 0$, so $-y \in G \cap N = 0$, a contradiction ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · exists_bound_below_on_compl⟧. A bounded-below operator from a Banach space has closed range (it is antilipschitz and uniformly continuous), and $(1+c)(G) = (1+c)(E)$ since $1 + c$ kills $N$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isClosed_range_one_add⟧.

**The range has finite codimension.** Suppose $R := \operatorname{range}(1 + c)$ has infinite codimension. Pick, for every proper subspace, a vector outside it ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · pick⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · pick_notMem⟧, and build the chain $M_0 = R$, $M_{j+1} = M_j + \mathbb R\,\mathrm{pick}(M_j)$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · chain⟧. Each $M_j$ is $R$ plus a finite-dimensional subspace ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · chain_eq_sup⟧, hence closed ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/FiniteDimension · Submodule.isClosed_sup_finiteDimensional⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · chain_isClosed⟧, and proper, since $R$ has infinite codimension ⟦Mathlib/RingTheory/Finiteness/Cofinite · FG.cofg_of_codisjoint⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · chain_ne_top⟧; the chain is increasing ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · chain_mono⟧. Riesz's lemma inside a subspace: for closed $M \subsetneq M'$ there is a unit vector of $M'$ at distance $\ge 1/2$ from $M$ (take $x \in M' \setminus M$, a point $y \in M$ with $\|x - y\| < 2\,\mathrm{dist}(x, M)$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/MetricSpace/HausdorffDistance · infDist_lt_iff⟧, and normalize $x - y$) ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · exists_unit_far⟧. This gives unit vectors $u_j \in M_{j+1}$ at distance $\ge 1/2$ from $M_j$. For $j < l$, $u_j + (1+c)u_l - (1+c)u_j \in M_l$, and $u_l - [u_j + (1+c)u_l - (1+c)u_j] = -(cu_l - cu_j)$, so $\|cu_l - cu_j\| \ge 1/2$. But the $cu_j$ lie in a compact set and so have a convergent subsequence, two of whose terms are within $1/2$ of each other: a contradiction ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · coFG_range_one_add⟧. Hence $1 + c$ is Fredholm ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_one_add⟧.

**Compact perturbations.** Let $u : E \to F$ be Fredholm and $k$ compact. Mathlib gives a quasi-inverse $v : F \to E$ of $u$, with $vu - 1$ and $uv - 1$ of finite rank ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Fredholm/Basic · isFredholm_iff_exists_isQuasiInverse⟧; a finite-rank difference from the identity is compact ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isCompactOperator_sub_one_of_equiv⟧. Then $v(u + k) = 1 + c_1$ and $(u + k)v = 1 + c_2$ with $c_1 = (vu - 1) + vk$ and $c_2 = (uv - 1) + kv$ compact. The kernel of $u + k$ lies in the finite-dimensional kernel of $1 + c_1$; the range of $u + k$ contains the range of $1 + c_2$, which is closed and of finite codimension, so $\operatorname{range}(u + k)$ has finite codimension ⟦Mathlib/RingTheory/Finiteness/Cofinite · CoFG.of_le⟧ and is closed, as a subspace containing a closed subspace of finite codimension ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/FiniteDimension · Submodule.isClosed_mono_of_finiteDimensional_quotient⟧. By the criterion, $u + k$ is Fredholm ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_add_isCompactOperator⟧.

**The index.** Mathlib has no index; it is built here, first for linear maps over a field. For $u : E \to F$ put $\operatorname{ind}(u) := \dim\ker u - \dim(F/\operatorname{range}u) \in \mathbb Z$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · linIndex⟧, meaningful when $u$ has *finite index*, i.e. finite-dimensional kernel and cokernel ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex⟧. *Additivity.* If $u : E \to F$ and $v : F \to G$ have finite index, so has $vu$, and $\operatorname{ind}(vu) = \operatorname{ind}(u) + \operatorname{ind}(v)$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.comp_and_linIndex⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.comp⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · linIndex_comp⟧. The proof is the dimension count along the exact sequence $0 \to \ker u \to \ker vu \to \operatorname{range}u \cap \ker v \to F/\operatorname{range}u \to G/\operatorname{range}vu \to G/\operatorname{range}v \to 0$, done as four applications of rank–nullity ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/FiniteDimensional/Lemmas · LinearMap.finrank_range_add_finrank_ker⟧ and two of the third isomorphism theorem ⟦Mathlib/LinearAlgebra/Isomorphisms · Submodule.quotientQuotientEquivQuotient⟧: to $u$ restricted to $\ker vu$ (kernel $\ker u$, range $\operatorname{range}u \cap \ker v$), to the quotient map $F \to F/\operatorname{range}u$ restricted to $\ker v$, and to $v$ followed by $G \to G/\operatorname{range}vu$ (kernel $\operatorname{range}u + \ker v$); finiteness of the new kernel and cokernel comes out of the same maps ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · finite_of_finite_ker_range⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · finite_of_finite_quotient⟧. Conversely, if $vu$ and $uv$ have finite index then so has $u$, since $\ker u \subseteq \ker vu$ and $\operatorname{range}uv \subseteq \operatorname{range}u$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · finiteDimensional_ker_of_comp⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · finiteDimensional_coker_of_comp⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.of_comp_of_comp⟧. *Index zero.* A linear equivalence has index $0$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.equiv⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · linIndex_equiv⟧. If $w - 1$ has finite-dimensional range $R$, then $\ker w \subseteq R$, $\operatorname{range}w + R = E$ and $R \cap \operatorname{range}w = w(R)$, so $\dim\ker w$ and $\dim(E/\operatorname{range}w)$ both equal $\dim R - \dim w(R)$ and $\operatorname{ind}(w) = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.of_finiteRange_sub_id_and_linIndex⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · linIndex_eq_zero_of_finiteRange_sub_id⟧. If $a$ is bijective and $r$ has finite rank, $a + r = a(1 + a^{-1}r)$ has index $0 + 0$ by additivity ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · HasFiniteIndex.of_bijective_add_and_linIndex⟧.

*Banach spaces.* For $u : E \to F$ continuous linear, the **Fredholm index** is $\operatorname{ind}(u)$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · fredholmIndex⟧; a Fredholm operator has finite index ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · IsFredholm.hasFiniteIndex⟧. *Small perturbations.* Let $v$ be a quasi-inverse of $u$ and $\|v\|\,\|s\| < 1$. Then $\|vs\|, \|sv\| < 1$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Basic · ContinuousLinearMap.opNorm_comp_le⟧, so $1 + vs$ and $1 + sv$ are invertible by the Neumann series ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/SpecificLimits/Normed · isUnit_one_sub_of_norm_lt_one⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · bijective_one_add_of_norm_lt⟧. Since $vu - 1$ and $uv - 1$ have finite rank ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · finiteDimensional_range_sub_id_of_equiv⟧, $v(u + s) = (1 + vs) + (vu - 1)$ and $(u + s)v = (1 + sv) + (uv - 1)$ are bijections plus finite-rank maps, hence of index $0$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · hasFiniteIndex_comp_add_of_norm_lt⟧; the same with $s = 0$ for $vu$ and $uv$. By the converse above, $u + s$, $u$ and $v$ have finite index, and additivity gives $\operatorname{ind}(u + s) + \operatorname{ind}(v) = 0 = \operatorname{ind}(u) + \operatorname{ind}(v)$, so $\operatorname{ind}(u + s) = \operatorname{ind}(u)$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · fredholmIndex_add_of_norm_lt⟧. *Compact perturbations.* Let $u$ be Fredholm and $k$ compact, and $\varphi(t) := \operatorname{ind}(u + tk)$ for $t \in \mathbb R$. At each $t_0$, $u + t_0k$ is Fredholm (the previous paragraph, $t_0k$ being compact ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Compact/Basic · IsCompactOperator.smul⟧), with a quasi-inverse $v_0$; for $t$ so close to $t_0$ that $\|v_0\|\,\|(t - t_0)k\| < 1$, $\varphi(t) = \operatorname{ind}((u + t_0k) + (t - t_0)k) = \varphi(t_0)$. ### I.8 Proposition 1.1.10 (preprint 1.9): sc-Fredholm operators are regularizing

**Statement.** Let $T$ have an sc-Fredholm splitting, $e \in E_0$, and $T_0 e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$. Then $e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · proposition_1_1_10⟧.

**Proof** (the text's). Write $T_0 e = \iota_m y$. Since $F_m = C_m \oplus Y_m$, $y = c + y'$ with $c \in C_m$ and $y' \in Y_m$, and $y' = T_m x'$ for some $x' \in X_m$ by the bijectivity of $T_m : X_m \to Y_m$. Then $T_0(e - \iota_m x') = \iota_m c$, so $\iota_m c$ lies in $C$ and in $Y = \operatorname{range} T_0$; these are complementary on level $0$, so $\iota_m c = 0$. Hence $e - \iota_m x' \in \ker T_0 = K \le E_\infty$ (§I.6), so it lifts to some $z \in E_m$, and $e = \iota_m(z + x')$. The step "$c = 0$" is where $C \cap Y = 0$ enters, as the exposition notes.

### I.9 Proposition 1.1.11 (preprint 1.10): compact perturbations of sc-Fredholm operators

**Statement.** If $T : E \to F$ is sc-Fredholm and $S : E \to F$ is an $\mathrm{sc}^+$-operator, then $T + S$ is sc-Fredholm ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · proposition_1_1_11_preprint⟧. Here $S$ is read as the sc-operator $E \to F$ underlying it (§I.2), and $T + S$ is the sum of sc-operators.

**The bootstrap.** The text says: if $y = (T + S)e \in Y_m$ then, since $T$ is regularizing, $e \in E_m$. But $Te = y - Se$ with $Se$ only one level up. The formal proof iterates: if $(T + S)_0 e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_{m+1}$, then a fortiori $(T+S)_0 e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScBanach.range_toBase_succ_le⟧, so $e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ by induction, so $S_0 e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_{m+1}$ (§I.2), so $T_0 e = (T+S)_0 e - S_0 e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_{m+1}$, and Proposition 1.1.10 (preprint 1.9) gives $e \in \operatorname{range}\iota_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScPlus.regularizing_add⟧. So $T + S$ is regularizing.

**The kernel.** Each $(T + S)_m = T_m + S_m$ is Fredholm, as a compact perturbation (§I.7) of the Fredholm operator $T_m$ (§I.6) ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_add_isCompactOperator⟧. Let $K' := \ker (T+S)_0$, finite-dimensional. Every $x \in K'$ is smooth: $(T+S)_0 x = 0 \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ for every $m$, so $x \in \operatorname{range}\iota_m$ by the bootstrap. By Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) there is an sc-splitting $E = K' \oplus X'$ ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.proposition_1_1_7⟧. (The text's $K_m = K_{m+1}$ is the same bootstrap.)

**The range.** Let $Y' := \operatorname{range}(T + S)_0$, closed since $(T+S)_0$ is Fredholm. The range of $(T+S)_m$ is exactly $Y'_m$: one inclusion from §I.6; conversely if $\iota_m y = (T+S)_0 e$ then $e$ lifts to level $m$ by the bootstrap, and $y = (T+S)_m e'$ by injectivity of $\iota_m$. $Y'$ is an sc-subspace: for $y = (T+S)_m e \in Y'_m$, approximate $e$ by smooth points $e_n \in E_m$; then $(T+S)_m e_n \to y$ and the $(T+S)_m e_n$ are smooth points of $Y'_m$ (sc-operators preserve smooth points, §I.2). This is the text's argument "$y_n = (T+S)e_n \in Y_\infty$, $y_n \to y$".

**The complement.** $F_0/Y'$ is finite-dimensional. Let $p : F_0 \to F_0/Y'$ be the quotient map. The image $p(F_\infty)$ is a subspace of the finite-dimensional $F_0/Y'$, dense because $F_\infty$ is dense in $F_0$ and $p$ is a continuous surjection, hence closed, hence everything. Instead of lifting a basis, take a linear right inverse $g$ of $p|_{F_\infty}$ and let $\sigma := F_\infty \hookrightarrow F_0$ composed with $g$, so $p \circ \sigma = \mathrm{id}$; put $C' := \operatorname{range}\sigma$, a finite-dimensional subspace of $F_\infty$. On every level $Y'_m$ and $C'_m$ are complementary: disjoint because an element of $C'_m \cap Y'_m$ is $\sigma(q)$ with $p\sigma(q) = q = 0$; spanning because for $z \in F_m$ the point $c := \sigma(p(\iota_m z))$ is smooth, so it lifts to level $m$, and $z - c \in Y'_m$. So $F = C' \oplus Y'$ is an sc-splitting ($C'$ is an sc-subspace by Proposition 1.1.7 (preprint 1.6) ⟦Chapter1/ScBanach · ScBanach.proposition_1_1_7_iff⟧).

**The bijections.** $(T+S)_m$ maps $X'_m$ into $Y'_m$; it is injective on $X'_m$ because its kernel on level $m$ is $K'_m$, which meets $X'_m$ in $0$; and it is onto $Y'_m$ because every $e \in E_m$ splits as $k + x$ with $k \in K'_m$, $x \in X'_m$ and $(T+S)_m e = (T+S)_m x$. This is condition (3) of Definition 1.1.9 (preprint 1.8), which the text does not verify; the exposition supplies it with the open mapping theorem, the formalization needs only the bijections (§I.6). Hence $T + S$ is sc-Fredholm ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · proposition_1_1_11_preprint⟧.

**The index.** The text does not say that the index is preserved; the exposition does. The index of $T + S$ is the Fredholm index of $(T + S)_0 = T_0 + S_0$ (§I.6); $T_0$ is Fredholm and $S_0$ compact, so by the invariance under compact perturbations (§I.7) $\operatorname{ind}(T + S) = \operatorname{ind}(T)$ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · proposition_1_1_11_index⟧. On every level, $T + S$ being sc-Fredholm, its level map $(T + S)_m$ is Fredholm of index $\operatorname{ind}(T + S) = \operatorname{ind}(T)$ by the index on every level (§I.6) ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · proposition_1_1_11_index_lvl⟧.

### I.10 Partial quadrants, tuples and sc-domains (Definition 1.1.12 (preprint 1.11))

**The quadrant.** In $\mathbb R^n \oplus W$, with $\mathbb R^n$ carrying its constant structure, the quadrant on level $m$ is the set of $(a, w)$ with $a_i \ge 0$ for all $i$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · quadrant⟧; it is convex ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · quadrant_convex⟧, closed ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · quadrant_isClosed⟧, has non-empty interior (the point $(1, \dots, 1, 0)$) ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · quadrant_interior_nonempty⟧, and the level sets of the level-$0$ quadrant are the level quadrants ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · lvlSet_quadrant⟧.

**Partial quadrants.** $C \subseteq E_0$ is a **partial quadrant** if $C = T_0^{-1}(\text{quadrant})$ for some $n$, some sc-Banach space $W$ and some sc-isomorphism $T : E \to \mathbb R^n \oplus W$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant⟧. The text asks in addition that $C$ be closed and convex; both follow. Indeed the level set $C_m$ is $T_m^{-1}$ of the level-$m$ quadrant ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.lvlSet_eq⟧, hence convex ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.convex_lvlSet⟧, closed ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.isClosed_lvlSet⟧ and with non-empty interior ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.interior_lvlSet_nonempty⟧; for $m = 0$ this is the text's "closed convex" ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.convex⟧ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.isClosed⟧. A convex set with non-empty interior has unique differentiability at every point of its closure ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/TangentCone/Real · uniqueDiffWithinAt_convex⟧, so $C_m$ has unique differentiability at each of its points ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsPartialQuadrant.uniqueDiffWithinAt_lvlSet⟧; this is the exposition's Lemma 3.D, that derivatives on a quadrant are unique, in Mathlib's language. The whole space is a partial quadrant with $n = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · isPartialQuadrant_univ⟧.

**Tuples.** $(U, C, E)$ is a tuple, $U$ a **relatively open subset of the partial quadrant $C$**, if $C$ is a partial quadrant and $U = O \cap C$ for an open $O \subseteq E_0$ ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsRelOpenInQuadrant⟧.

**Sc-domains.** $U \subseteq E_0$ is an **sc-domain** if for every $m$ the level set $U_m$ is the intersection of an open and a convex subset of $E_m$, and $U_m$ has unique differentiability at each of its points ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsScDomain⟧. The property passes to the tangent: $(TU)_m = U_{m+1} \times E_m$ (§I.11) is $(O \times E_m) \cap (C \times E_m)$, and unique differentiability of a product is the product of the two ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsScDomain.tangentSet⟧; and to the level shift, since the level sets of $U_1$ in $E^1$ are the $U_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsScDomain.shift⟧. This is what the proofs of §§1.2–1.3 use in place of the text's claim that $T(U, C, E)$ is again a tuple (Definition 1.1.14 (preprint 1.13)). Every theorem of §§1.2–1.3 below is stated for sc-domains; the tuples are a special case.

**The shift and the tangent of a tuple (Lemmas 3.A(2) and 3.B of the exposition).** The text's claim is nevertheless true, and is proved with Chapter 2, where retracts need it. ### I.12 Theorem 1.3.1 (preprint 1.21): the chain rule

**Statement.** Let $U \subseteq C \subseteq E$ and $V \subseteq D \subseteq F$ be relatively open subsets of partial quadrants, $f$ $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $U$ with $f(U) \subseteq V$, and $g$ $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $V$. ### I.13 The compact-open lemma and the level operators $Df(x) : E_m \to F_m$

The proofs of Propositions 1.2.1–1.2.3 (preprint 1.16–1.18) in the 2010 paper all rest on one mechanism, which the formal proof isolates.

**The lemma, $k = 0$.** Let $A : X \to \mathcal L(Y, Z)$ with $(x, y) \mapsto A(x)y$ continuous on $s \times Y$, and $\iota : Y' \to Y$ a compact operator. Then $x \mapsto A(x) \circ \iota$ is continuous on $s$ into $\mathcal L(Y', Z)$ with the operator norm ⟦Chapter1/Levels · continuousOn_clm_comp_of_isCompactOperator⟧. Proof: the image of the unit ball of $Y'$ under $\iota$ lies in a compact $K$; by uniform continuity on compact sets ⟦Chapter1/Levels · exists_delta_of_continuousOn⟧, for $x' \in s$ near $x$ and all $w \in K$, $\|A(x')w - A(x)w\| \le \varepsilon/2$, so $\|(A(x') - A(x)) \circ \iota\| \le \varepsilon/2$ on unit vectors. This is the 2010 paper's subsequence argument (unit vectors $h_n \in E_{m+1}$ have a subsequence converging in $E_m$) in uniform form.

**The lemma, $C^k$.** Let $s$ be open-in-convex with unique differentiability at every point, and $(x, y) \mapsto A(x)y$ of class $C^k$ on $s \times Y$. Then $x \mapsto A(x) \circ \iota$ is $C^k$ on $s$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · contDiffOn_clm_comp_of_isCompactOperator⟧. Induction on $k$ over all $Z$ and $A$. For $k + 1$: the partial derivative in $x$, $B(x)y := D_x(A(x)y)$, is $C^k$ in $(x, y)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · ContDiffOn⟧, linear and continuous in $y$ for $x \in s$ (differentiate the linearity identities and use uniqueness of derivatives), so $B : s \to \mathcal L(Y, \mathcal L(X, Z))$; by the induction hypothesis $x \mapsto B(x) \circ \iota$ is $C^k$, and so is its flip $G'(x) \in \mathcal L(X, \mathcal L(Y', Z))$. $G'$ is the derivative of $x \mapsto A(x) \circ \iota$: for $z \in s$ near $x$ the segment from $x$ to $z$ lies in $s$ (open-in-convex), and for a unit vector $v \in Y'$ the mean value inequality applied to $\tau \mapsto A(x + \tau(z - x))(\iota v) - \tau B(x)(\iota v)(z - x)$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/MeanValue · Convex.norm_image_sub_le_of_norm_hasDerivWithin_le⟧, with the uniform continuity of $B$ on $\{x\} \times \iota(\bar B_1)$, bounds $\|A(z)\iota v - A(x)\iota v - B(x)(\iota v)(z - x)\| \le c\|z - x\|$ uniformly in $v$. So $x \mapsto A(x)\circ\iota$ is differentiable within $s$ with a $C^k$ derivative, hence $C^{k+1}$ ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/ContDiff/Defs · contDiffOn_succ_iff_hasFDerivWithinAt_of_uniqueDiffOn⟧. The 2010 paper's Proposition 2.3 carries the same information in the multilinear maps $\Gamma(x)(h_1, \dots, h_k, h)$ and a differentiation under the integral sign; the lemma replaces both.

**The level operators.** Let $f$ be $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $U$ with derivatives $D$, and $x \in E_{m+1}$. The **level-$m$ operator** $Df(x) : E_m \to F_m$ is the continuous linear $T$ with $\iota_m \circ T = D(\iota x) \circ \iota_m$, when such $T$ exists (it is unique by injectivity), and $0$ otherwise ⟦Chapter1/Levels · levelDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · toBase_levelDeriv⟧; on level $0$ it is $D(x)$ itself ⟦Chapter1/Levels · levelDeriv_zero⟧. For $x \in U_{m+1}$ it exists: the level-$m$ tangent map sends $(x, v) \in U_{m+1} \times E_m$ to a pair whose second component $T_m(v)$ satisfies $\iota_m T_m(v) = D(\iota x)\iota_m v$; $T_m$ is linear (by injectivity of $\iota_m$) and continuous (condition (3) on level $m$) ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.exists_levelDeriv⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.toBase_levelDeriv⟧. So the level-$m$ tangent map is $(x, h) \mapsto (f_{m+1}(x), Df(x)h)$ on $U_{m+1} \times E_m$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.levelMap_tangentMapWith⟧, and condition (3) says exactly that $(x, h) \mapsto Df(x)h$ is continuous on $U_{m+1} \times E_m$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.continuousOn_levelDeriv⟧: this is condition (2) of Proposition 1.2.1 (preprint 1.16). By the compact-open lemma with $\iota = \mathrm{incl}_m : E_{m+1} \to E_m$, the map $x \mapsto Df(x) \circ \mathrm{incl}_m$ is continuous $U_{m+1} \to \mathcal L(E_{m+1}, F_m)$ in the operator norm ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.continuousOn_levelDeriv_clm⟧: the continuity of $df$ in condition (1) of Proposition 1.2.1 (preprint 1.16). The base case, $x \mapsto D(x) \circ \iota_1$ continuous $U_1 \to \mathcal L(E_1, F_0)$, is also proved directly ⟦Chapter1/Properties · IsSc1With.continuousOn_deriv_clm⟧, and with it $f \circ \iota_1 : U_1 \to F_0$ is $C^1$ ⟦Chapter1/Properties · IsSc1With.contDiffOn_base⟧.

**Level-raising: differentiability of $f : U_{m+1} \to F_m$.** Let $U$ be an sc-domain. For $x \in U_{m+1}$, the map $f_m \circ \mathrm{incl}_m : U_{m+1} \to F_m$ has derivative $Df(x) \circ \mathrm{incl}_m$ at $x$ within $U_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.hasFDerivWithinAt_level⟧. This is the 2010 paper's integral representation $f(x+k) - f(x) = \int_0^1 Df(x + \tau k)k\,d\tau$. For $z \in U_{m+1}$ near $x$ and $k := z - x$, the segment $x + \tau k$ lies in $U_{m+1}$ (open-in-convex). The integrand $g(\tau) := Df(x + \tau k)(\mathrm{incl}_m k)$ is continuous into $F_m$ by condition (3) on level $m$, so its Bochner integral over $[0, 1]$ exists in $F_m$. Its image under $\iota_m$ is the integral of $D(\iota(x + \tau k))\iota_{m+1}k$ in $F_0$, which by the fundamental theorem of calculus for the $C^1$ curve $\tau \mapsto f(\iota(x + \tau k))$ (condition (2) for $f$, composed with the affine curve) ⟦Mathlib/MeasureTheory/Integral/IntervalIntegral/FundThmCalculus · integral_eq_sub_of_hasDerivAt_of_le⟧ equals $f(\iota z) - f(\iota x) = \iota_m(f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m z) - f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x))$. By injectivity, $f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m z) - f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x) = \int_0^1 g$ in $F_m$. Then
$$\Big\|f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m z) - f_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x) - Df(x)\mathrm{incl}_m k\Big\|_m \le \sup_{\tau} \|(Df(x + \tau k) - Df(x)) \circ \mathrm{incl}_m\|\,\|k\|_{m+1},$$
and the supremum tends to $0$ by the norm continuity just proved. The 2010 paper forms the integral in $F_0$ and remarks that the integrand is continuous into $F_m$; here it is formed in $F_m$ and pushed down. Consequently $f_m \circ \mathrm{incl}_m : U_{m+1} \to F_m$ is $C^1$ within $U_{m+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.contDiffOn_level⟧, which is condition (1) of Proposition 1.2.1 (preprint 1.16) in full.

**The level operator is the derivative of $f_m$.** If $f_m : U_m \to F_m$ happens to be differentiable at $\mathrm{incl}_m x$ within $U_m$ with derivative $L$, then $L = Df(x)$: both $\iota_m \circ L \circ \mathrm{incl}_m$ and $D(\iota x) \circ \iota_{m+1}$ are derivatives of $f \circ \iota_{m+1}$ at $x$ within $U_{m+1}$, hence equal by uniqueness; and since $\mathrm{incl}_m$ has dense range, $\iota_m \circ L = D(\iota x) \circ \iota_m$ on all of $E_m$ ⟦Mathlib/Topology/Algebra/Module/ContinuousLinearMap/Basic · ext_on⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.levelDeriv_eq⟧. This is the exposition's Lemma 3.H and the key to Proposition 1.2.4 (preprint 1.19).

### I.17 Proposition 1.2.4 (preprint 1.19) and Corollary 1.2.5 (preprint 1.20): sc^{k+1} from classical smoothness on the levels

**Statement.** Let $U$ be an sc-domain and $f$ $\mathrm{sc}^0$ on $U$. If for every $m \ge 0$ and $0 \le l \le k$ the map $f_m \circ (E_{m+l} \to E_m) : U_{m+l} \to F_m$ is $C^{l+1}$ within $U_{m+l}$, then $f$ is $\mathrm{sc}^{k+1}$ on $U$ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · proposition_1_2_4_text⟧. This is the text's statement; its "$f$ induces a map $f : U_{m+l} \to F_m$" is the $\mathrm{sc}^0$ hypothesis (values of level $m$ on $U_m$; the continuity is then the case $l = 0$). The proof is the 2010 paper's (its Proposition 2.4) and lives in `Chapter1/LevelsWeak`; an earlier, shorter proof under a stronger hypothesis is kept and described at the end of this section.

**Why the hypothesis does not induct.** The obvious induction would show that $f$ is $\mathrm{sc}^1$ and that $Tf$ satisfies the hypothesis with $k - 1$. It does not. The map $Tf : (TU)_{m+l} \to (TF)_m$ has the second component $(x, h) \mapsto Df(x)h$ on $U_{m+l+1} \times E_{m+l} \to F_m$, and $Df(x)$ on $E_{m+l}$ is the derivative of $f : U_{m+l} \to F_m$ (§I.13), a $C^{l+1}$ map; so this component is only $C^l$, one order short of the $C^{l+1}$ that the hypothesis for $Tf$ would ask. What survives the induction is a description of the components of the iterated tangent maps: they are built from the derivatives $d^jf(x)$ of $f$ itself, evaluated at points and vectors of various levels, and the regularity of $f$ on the coarser levels supplies the missing order. The 2010 paper's inductive statement $(\mathrm S_k)$ carries exactly this description. The formal proof organizes it in six steps: (1) the derivatives $d^jf(x)$ on all level pairs, with their compatibility, form a *derivative tower*; (2) an *elementary map* is $d^jf$ evaluated at sc-operators; (3) it is $\mathrm{sc}^0$, and $\mathrm{sc}^1$ with an explicit derivative, by the level criterion of Proposition 1.2.1 (preprint 1.16); (4) its tangent map is the pair of an elementary map and a sum of elementary maps; (5) by induction on $s$, every elementary map with $j + s \le k + 1$ is $\mathrm{sc}^s$; (6) $f$ is the elementary map with $j = 0$.

**The derivative tower.** A derivative tower of order $k + 1$ for $f$ on $U$ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · IsDerivTower⟧ consists of base-level maps $\Phi_i(x)(h_1, \dots, h_i) \in F_0$ ($x, h_a \in E_0$) and, for $x \in U_{m+l}$, continuous $i$-linear maps $\Psi_{i,m,l}(x)$ on $E_{m+l}$ with values in $F_m$, given for $i \le l + 1$ and $i \le k + 1$, such that: $\Phi_0 = f$; $\iota_m \Psi_{i,m,l}(x)(h_1, \dots, h_i) = \Phi_i(\iota_{m+l}x)(\iota_{m+l}h_1, \dots, \iota_{m+l}h_i)$, i.e. all the $\Psi$ are one map seen on different levels; $\Psi_{i,m,l}$ is continuous on $U_{m+l}$; and for $i \le l$, $i \le k$ it is differentiable within $U_{m+l}$ at every point, with derivative $\xi \mapsto \Psi_{i+1,m,l}(x)(\xi, \cdot)$. The ranges of $i$ are exactly what "$f : U_{m+l} \to F_m$ is $C^{l+1}$" provides; the compatibility is the text's use of one symbol $d^jf(x)$ on all levels. **Elementary maps.** Elementary data on an sc-Banach space $X$ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData⟧ are an sc-operator $\pi_0 : X \to E^p$ (the base point), $i$ sc-operators $\pi_a : X \to E^{n_a}$ (the vectors) and a target shift $m$. The elementary map is

$$m + i \le p, \qquad m + i \le n_a + 1 \ (1 \le a \le i), \qquad i \le k .$$

The first is $j + l \le k + 1$ for the base point $x \in U_{k+1}$, which the text derives from $j + l - 1 \le n_i \le k$ (and states separately, as $l \le k + 1$, only for $j = 0$ in the exposition). On level $l$, the base point and the vectors pushed down to a common level $q$ ($q \le p + l$, $q \le n_a + l$) are $\rho_0 = (E_{p+l} \to E_q) \circ \pi_0$ and $\rho_a = (E_{n_a+l} \to E_q) \circ \pi_a$ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.ρ₀⟧ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.ρ⟧. **The sc¹ step.** For $z \in W_{l+1}$ the candidate derivative $Dg(z) : X_l \to F_{m+l}$ is the derivative (3.5) of the exposition ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.deriv⟧ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.deriv_apply⟧:
$$Dg(z)\zeta = \Psi_{i+1,m+l,i}(\rho_0 z)(\rho_0\zeta, \rho_1 z, \dots, \rho_i z) + \sum_{a=1}^i \Psi_{i,m+l,i-1}(\rho_0 z)(\rho_1 z, \dots, \rho_a\zeta, \dots, \rho_i z),$$

*Differentiability of $g : W_{l+1} \to F_{m+l}$ with derivative $Dg(z)$ restricted to $X_{l+1}$.* On $W_{l+1}$, the level-$l$ map of $g$ is $z' \mapsto \Psi_{i,m+l,i}(\rho_0 z')(\rho_1 z', \dots, \rho_i z')$ with the slots taken at level $l + 1$ (the formula above with $\lambda = i$) ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.levelMap_incl_eq⟧. *$k + 1$ from $k$.* Suppose every $f_m$ is $C^{k+2}$. Then $f$ is $\mathrm{sc}^1$ by the case $k = 0$, with the derivative $Df$ above, and we must show $Tf$ is $\mathrm{sc}^{k+1}$ on $TU$. By the induction hypothesis (for the sc-domain $TU$ and the $\mathrm{sc}^0$ map $Tf$) it suffices that the level maps of $Tf$ are $C^{k+1}$. The level-$m$ map of $Tf$ is $(x, h) \mapsto (f_{m+1}(x), Df(x)h)$ with the level operator (§I.13), and the level operator at $x \in U_{m+1}$ is the derivative of $f_m$ at $\mathrm{incl}_m x$ (Lemma 3.H, ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1With.levelDeriv_eq⟧). So the level-$m$ map of $Tf$ is $(x, h) \mapsto (f_{m+1}(x), df_m(\mathrm{incl}_m x)h)$ on $U_{m+1} \times E_m$, whose components are $C^{k+1}$: the first as $f_{m+1}$ is, the second as the derivative of a $C^{k+2}$ map is $C^{k+1}$ and evaluation is bilinear ⟦Chapter1/Levels · proposition_1_2_4⟧. This is where the stronger hypothesis is used, and where the text's hypothesis would fail (see "Why the hypothesis does not induct").

*Sc-smooth, and the corollary.* If $f$ maps each $U_m$ into $F_m$ and every $f_m$ is $C^\infty$, then $f$ is sc-smooth ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsScSmooth.of_contDiffOn_levels⟧. For a finite-dimensional target: $C^1$ on every $U_m$ gives $\mathrm{sc}^1$ ⟦Chapter1/Properties · corollary_1_2_5_zero⟧, $C^{k+1}$ on every $U_m$ gives $\mathrm{sc}^{k+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · corollary_1_2_5⟧, in particular $C^{k+1}$ on $U = U_0$ does, since restricting along $\iota_m$ preserves $C^{k+1}$ ⟦Chapter1/Levels · corollary_1_2_5_base⟧.

### I.18 Definition 1.3.3 (preprint 1.23) and Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24): the degeneracy index

**Definitions.** For $p = (a, w) \in \mathbb R^n \oplus W$ let $\#(p)$ be the number of indices $i$ with $a_i = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · quadrantDeg⟧. For an sc-isomorphism $T : E \to \mathbb R^n \oplus W$ presenting a partial quadrant $C$ and $x \in E_0$, let $d_T(x) := \#(T_0 x)$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · degIndexWith⟧. The **degeneracy index** $d_C(x)$ is $d_T(x)$ for a presentation $T$ of $C$ chosen once and for all, and $0$ if $C$ is not a partial quadrant ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · degIndex⟧; by Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24) below it equals $d_T(x)$ for every presentation and every $x \in C$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · degIndex_eq⟧, and on the quadrant itself it is $\#$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · degIndex_quadrant⟧. The text's interior points, boundary points and corners are the points with $d_C = 0$, $1$, $\ge 2$; Chapter 2 makes the boundary a definition.

**Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24), core form.** Let $S : \mathbb R^n \times W_0 \to \mathbb R^{n'} \times W'_0$ be a continuous linear equivalence with $q \in Q \iff Sq \in Q'$ for the two quadrants. Then $\#(p) = \#(Sp)$ for every $p \in Q$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · quadrantDeg_eq_of_equiv⟧. Only level $0$ and linear algebra are used; the sc-structure plays no role. Proof. For $p \in Q$ let $E_p := \{(b, v) : b_i = 0 \text{ whenever } p_i = 0\}$, the kernel of the surjection $(b, v) \mapsto (b_i)_{i \in I(p)}$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · coordMap⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · coordMap_surjective⟧ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · mem_ker_coordMap⟧, so that $\dim(V/E_p) = \#(p)$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · finrank_quotient_ker_coordMap⟧: this makes the text's "codimension of $E_x$" precise. For $u \in E_p$, $p + tu \in Q$ for all $t$ near $0$: coordinate by coordinate, if $p_i = 0$ then $u_i = 0$ and the coordinate stays $0$; if $p_i > 0$ it stays positive for small $t$ by continuity ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · eventually_add_smul_mem_quadrant⟧. Hence $Sp + tSu \in Q'$ for $t$ near $0$, so for each $i$ with $(Sp)_i = 0$ we have $0 \le t(Su)_i$ for all $t$ near $0$, of both signs, which forces $(Su)_i = 0$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · eq_zero_of_eventually_nonneg_mul⟧; so $S(E_p) \subseteq E'_{Sp}$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · map_ker_coordMap_le⟧. The same argument for $S^{-1}$ gives the reverse inclusion (the text concludes equality from one inclusion "since $S$ is an isomorphism", which for infinite-dimensional $W$ needs this second step), so $S(E_p) = E'_{Sp}$ and $S$ induces a linear isomorphism $V/E_p \cong V'/E'_{Sp}$; comparing dimensions, $\#(p) = \#(Sp)$.

**Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24).** If $T$ and $T'$ both present $C$, then $S := T'_0 \circ T_0^{-1}$ carries the quadrant onto the quadrant (both are the image of $C$), and the core form at $p = T_0 x$ gives $d_T(x) = d_{T'}(x)$ for $x \in C$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · lemma_1_3_4⟧. The text proves first that $k = k'$; here this is a consequence: $T_0^{-1}(0) \in C$ and $\#(0) = n$ in $\mathbb R^n \oplus W$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · quadrantDeg_zero⟧, so the lemma at this point gives $n = n'$ ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · lemma_1_3_4_dim⟧.

### I.19 Theorem 1.3.5 (preprint 1.25): boundary recognition

**Statement.** Let $U \subseteq C \subseteq E$ and $U' \subseteq C' \subseteq E'$ be relatively open subsets of partial quadrants, $f$ $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $U$ with $f(U) \subseteq U'$, $g$ $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $U'$ with $g(U') \subseteq U$, $g \circ f = \mathrm{id}$ on $U$ and $f \circ g = \mathrm{id}$ on $U'$. Then $d_C(x) = d_{C'}(f(x))$ for every $x \in U$ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · theorem_1_3_5⟧. The text's "germ of a local sc-diffeomorphism" is undefined in Chapter 1 and is read this way, with $U$, $U'$ as small as one likes; the text has sc-smooth where $\mathrm{sc}^1$ suffices, so the formal statement is stronger. The text gives no proof, referring to "[20], Theorem 1.19"; the formal proof is the argument of §2.4 (preprint §2.3) (Lemma 2.4.9 (preprint 2.29), Theorem 2.4.12 (preprint 2.31), Corollary 2.4.13 (preprint 2.32)) specialized to the identity retraction, and lives in the Chapter 2 file because it uses the retraction vocabulary. Smooth points are not needed; level-$1$ points suffice.

**The subspaces $E_x$.** For a presentation $T$ of $C$ and $x \in E_0$, let $E_x := \{v \in E_0 : (T_0 v)_i = 0 \text{ whenever } (T_0 x)_i = 0\}$, the kernel of a continuous linear map to a finite-dimensional space ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · minimalSubspaceWith⟧; its codimension is $d_T(x)$ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · finrank_quotient_minimalSubspaceWith⟧. Two facts about the index: it is upper semicontinuous, $d_T(y) \le d_T(x)$ for $y$ near $x$ (a nonzero coordinate stays nonzero) ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · eventually_degIndexWith_le⟧; and every $x \in C$ is the limit of points of $C$ on level $1$ with the same index, namely $T_0^{-1}(a, w_n)$ with $w_n \in W_1$ converging to $w$ in $W_0$, which exist by density ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · exists_tendsto_lvl1_degIndexWith⟧. These are the exposition's Lemma 5.A, with level $1$ in place of smooth.

**The sign argument.** Let $f$ be $\mathrm{sc}^1$ on $U \subseteq C$ with values in $C'$, $x \in U_1$, and $v \in E_1$ with $\iota_1 v \in E_x$. Then $x + tv \in U_1$ for $t$ near $0$ (the quadrant coordinates as in §I.18, and the openness of $U_1$) ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · eventually_add_smul_mem_lvlSet⟧, so $f(\iota(x + tv)) \in C'$ and, for each $j$ with $(T'_0 f(x))_j = 0$, the function $t \mapsto (T'_0 f(\iota(x + tv)))_j$ is $\ge 0$ with a zero at $t = 0$: a local minimum, so its derivative at $0$, which is $(T'_0 Df(x)\iota_1 v)_j$ by condition (2), vanishes ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Calculus/LocalExtr/Basic · IsLocalMin.hasDerivAt_eq_zero⟧ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · IsSc1With.deriv_mem_minimalSubspace_of_lvl1⟧. So $Df(x)$ maps $E_x \cap \iota_1(E_1)$ into $E'_{f(x)}$. Since $f$ is differentiable only along $E_1$, the density of $E_x \cap \iota_1(E_1)$ in $E_x$ is needed to conclude $Df(x)(E_x) \subseteq E'_{f(x)}$; it holds because $E_x$ is the kernel of a continuous linear map to a finite-dimensional space and $\iota_1(E_1)$ is dense ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · ScBanach.subset_closure_ker_inter_range_toBase⟧ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · IsSc1With.mapsTo_minimalSubspace⟧. The text asserts the corresponding density ("the closure of $(T^R_xO)_\infty$ is $T^R_xO$") without proof.

**One inequality at level-$1$ points.** With $g$ the inverse, the chain rule gives $Dg(f_1(x)) \circ Df(x) = \mathrm{id}$ on $E_0$ at $x \in U_1$ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · scDeriv_comp_eq_self⟧, so $Df(x)$ is injective and $E_x \cong Df(x)(E_x) \le E'_{f(x)}$ sits inside a subspace of codimension $d_{C'}(f(x))$, while $Dg(f_1(x))$ maps $E'_{f(x)}$ into $E_x$ by the sign argument for $g$; comparing codimensions, $d_C(x) \le d_{C'}(f(x))$ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · degIndexWith_le_of_germ⟧. Applying this to $g$ at $f_1(x)$ gives the reverse inequality, so $d_C(x) = d_{C'}(f(x))$ for $x \in U_1$ ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · theorem_1_3_5_lvl1⟧.

**General points.** For $x \in U$, approximate $x$ by level-$1$ points $x_n \in U$ of $C$ with $d_C(x_n) = d_C(x)$; then $f(x_n) \to f(x)$ (continuity of $f$ on $U$), so $d_{C'}(f(x_n)) \le d_{C'}(f(x))$ eventually by semicontinuity, and $d_{C'}(f(x_n)) = d_C(x_n) = d_C(x)$ by the level-$1$ case; hence $d_C(x) \le d_{C'}(f(x))$. The symmetric argument for $g$ at $f(x)$ gives equality ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · theorem_1_3_5⟧.

### I.20 What the formalization taught us

- **The chain rule needs one integral less than the text writes, and one hypothesis more than it states.** The term $Dg(f(x))[f(x+h) - f(x) - Df(x)h]$ is $o(|h|_1)$ because $Dg(f(x))$ is bounded, with no integral ⟦Chapter1/ChainRule · IsSc1With.hasFDerivWithinAt_comp⟧; the segment from $f(x)$ to $f(x+h)$ lies in $V_1$ only because $V_1$ is open-in-convex and $f : U_1 \to F_1$ is continuous, which the text does not say. The whole proof uses, of the quadrant structure of $U$, only the uniqueness of derivatives, and that only for $T(g \circ f) = Tg \circ Tf$ ⟦Chapter1/ChainRule · theorem_1_3_1'⟧.
- **"The" derivative $Df(x)$ is a theorem, not a notation.** It is unique because $E_1$ is dense in $E_0$ and $U_1$ has unique differentiability ⟦Chapter1/ScSmooth · IsSc1With.eq_of_uniqueDiffWithinAt⟧; without it the tangent map, hence $\mathrm{sc}^2$, is undefined. The same density argument shows that the extensions $Df(x) : E_{m-1} \to F_{m-1}$ of Proposition 1.2.1 (preprint 1.16), written with one symbol in the text, are restrictions of one operator ⟦Chapter1/Levels · IsSc1Levels.toBase_apply⟧, which is what makes the "clear" direction of that proposition work.
- **One compactness argument carries §1.2.** The compact-open lemma ⟦Chapter1/Levels · contDiffOn_clm_comp_of_isCompactOperator⟧ turns joint continuity (or $C^k$-ness) of $(x, h) \mapsto Df(x)h$ into norm continuity (or $C^k$-ness) of $x \mapsto Df(x)|_{E_{m+1}}$; Propositions 1.2.1, 1.2.2 and 1.2.3 (preprint 1.16, 1.17 and 1.18) are this lemma plus bookkeeping of levels.
- **Proposition 1.2.4 (preprint 1.19) does not induct on its own hypothesis.** If $f$ satisfies it with $k$, then $Tf$ in general does not satisfy it with $k - 1$: the second component $(x, h) \mapsto Df(x)h : U_{m+l+1} \times E_{m+l} \to F_m$ is one order short. The stronger hypothesis "$C^{k+1}$ on every level" makes the induction two lines ⟦Chapter1/Levels · proposition_1_2_4⟧; the text's needs the 2010 paper's $(\mathrm S_k)$, which remembers $f$ through the elementary maps $d^jf$ on mixed levels ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.isSck⟧ ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · proposition_1_2_4_text⟧. Written out, $(\mathrm S_k)$ needs three things the text leaves implicit: one $d^jf(x)$ on all levels ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · isDerivTower_of_contDiffOn_levels⟧, the base-point condition $j + l \le k + 1$ in place of the bound $n_i \le k$ (which is not needed) ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · ElemData.Good⟧, and the derivative of the multilinear evaluation ⟦Chapter1/LevelsWeak · hasFDerivAt_cmm_eval⟧.
- **Regularizing is a bootstrap.** In §1.4.1 the step "$e \in E_m$ by Proposition 1.9" needs $m$ applications, since $Se$ is only one level above $e$ ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · ScPlus.regularizing_add⟧.
- **The index is an addition, and an easy one once additivity is there.** Neither Proposition 1.1.11 (preprint 1.10) nor the remark after Definition 1.1.9 (preprint 1.8) mentions the index of $T + S$ or of the level operators; with the additivity of the index ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · linIndex_comp⟧ and a quasi-inverse, the index is locally constant along $u + tk$, hence invariant ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · fredholmIndex_add_isCompactOperator⟧, and the sc-statements follow on every level ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · proposition_1_1_11_index_lvl⟧.
- **The text's quick remarks are all true, and some need real arguments.** "Precisely one sc-structure" in finite dimensions ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isoConstOfFiniteDimensional⟧; "$(E/A)_\infty$ consists of the classes of $E_\infty$", a Mittag-Leffler series ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.exists_smooth_mem_coset⟧; "it is a sc-projection" ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · ScBanach.isScSplitting_iff_exists_scProj⟧; "sc¹ is $C^1$ in finite dimensions", which at the boundary of a quadrant means derivatives within $U$ ⟦Chapter1/ExpositionLemmas · isSc1_iff_fderivWithin_const⟧.
- **Mathlib had Fredholm operators but not Riesz–Schauder.** The theorem that $1 + \text{compact}$ is Fredholm, and the stability under compact perturbations, had to be proved ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_one_add⟧ ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · isFredholm_add_isCompactOperator⟧; the finite codimension of the range comes from a chain of closed subspaces and Riesz's lemma ⟦Chapter1/FredholmPerturbation · coFG_range_one_add⟧.
- **The degeneracy index knows nothing of the sc-structure.** Lemma 1.3.4 (preprint 1.24) holds for any linear equivalence of the base levels carrying one quadrant onto the other ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · quadrantDeg_eq_of_equiv⟧, and $k = k'$ is its value at the origin ⟦Chapter1/Degeneracy · lemma_1_3_4_dim⟧. Boundary recognition needs only $\mathrm{sc}^1$ and only level-$1$ points, not smooth ones ⟦Chapter2/DegeneracyIndex · theorem_1_3_5⟧.

### I.21 The 2021 edition: Remark 1.1.2 and Proposition 1.1.11 with the index

The Springer edition (2021) changes Chapter 1 in two places. Remark 1.1.2 (p. 5) is new, and the statement of Proposition 1.1.11 (p. 9; preprint 1.10) now includes the index.

**Remark 1.1.2.** The remark makes "Banach space" mean a *Banachable* space, a vector space whose topology is induced by some complete norm, and says that all norms inducing the topology are equivalent "in view of the open mapping theorem". The formalization models one vector space with two norms as two normed spaces $E_1$, $E_2$ and a linear equivalence $e : E_1 \to E_2$, the identity of the underlying space. It records the remark's content in two readings. *(1) Same topology, equivalent norms* ⟦Chapter1/Book2021 · remark_1_1_2⟧. If $e$ and $e^{-1}$ are continuous, they are bounded, $\|e x\| \le \|f\|\,\|x\|$ and $\|x\| = \|g(e x)\| \le \|g\|\,\|e x\|$ for the continuous linear maps $f$, $g$ they define, and $c = \max(\|f\|, \|g\|) + 1$ serves for both inequalities. No completeness enters. *(2) The open mapping theorem* ⟦Chapter1/Book2021 · remark_1_1_2_openMapping⟧. If both norms are complete and $e$ is continuous, the bijective continuous linear map $e$ is a linear homeomorphism by the open mapping theorem ⟦Mathlib/Analysis/Normed/Operator/Banach · ContinuousLinearEquiv.ofBijective⟧. So $e^{-1}$ is continuous, and (1) applies. As we read it, the sentence of the remark is true without the open mapping theorem. The theorem is what makes two complete norms equivalent when one of them dominates the other.

**Proposition 1.1.11 (preprint 1.10), 2021 statement.** Let $T : E \to F$ be sc-Fredholm and $S : E \to F$ an sc$^+$-operator. Then $T + S$ is sc-Fredholm, $\operatorname{ind}(T + S) = \operatorname{ind}(T)$, and every level map $(T + S)_m : E_m \to F_m$ is Fredholm of index $\operatorname{ind}(T)$ ⟦Chapter1/Book2021 · proposition_1_1_11⟧. Nothing new has to be proved, because the formal statement is the conjunction of three earlier theorems:
- the sc-Fredholm property of §I.9 ⟦Chapter1/ScFredholm · proposition_1_1_11_preprint⟧;
- the index on level $0$, from the invariance of the Fredholm index under compact perturbations ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · proposition_1_1_11_index⟧;
- the index on every level ⟦Chapter1/FredholmIndex · proposition_1_1_11_index_lvl⟧.

The 2021 proof in Appendix 1.4.1 is the earlier one and does not argue the index. The index statement, the exposition's addition in the earlier editions, is thus now the book's own. The level statement remains the formalization's.
